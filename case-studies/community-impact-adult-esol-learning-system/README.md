# Adult ESOL Learning System at Columbia Community Impact (2018–2020)

## Case study summary

From 2018 to 2020, Laura Elizabeth Hand served as ESOL Lead Teacher Coordinator and instructor with Community Impact at Columbia University. The role combined curriculum development, instructor recruitment and support, assessment, staffing, professional development, program operations, and adult-learning pedagogy across a multi-site ESOL program.

The surviving archive documents this as a learning **system**, not a single course or textbook.

## Program scope

The surviving CV and program materials support the following scope:

- adult ESOL programming across five primary instructional sites;
- roughly 85–110 volunteer and part-time instructors per 12-week cycle;
- interviewing, onboarding, scheduling, substitute coverage, training, observation, and ongoing instructor support;
- literacy testing and learner-progress/retention analysis;
- curriculum planning and resource distribution across up to roughly 70 courses per cycle;
- integration of career-readiness content into existing ESOL materials;
- recurring professional development for instructors;
- rapid adaptation of curriculum and delivery during the Spring 2020 COVID disruption.

## The Level 6 curriculum

A surviving Summer 2019 Level 6 booklet contains **10 chapters and 40 lessons** across more than 329 pages.

Its sequence moves through:

1. orientation, introductions, listening, and goal-setting;
2. career sectors, career families, technology, and the labor market;
3. career research, traits, and employer types;
4. career maps, résumés, and cover letters;
5. informational interviewing, career narratives, goal review, and job ads;
6. mock interviews, job-offer decisions, peer review, and workplace expectations;
7. workplace communication, entrepreneurship, and generational issues;
8. workers' rights, child development, paid family leave, and debate;
9. family literacy and intergenerational education;
10. high-school equivalency, CUNY pathways, program requirements, and next steps.

## Content and language were paired

The curriculum repeatedly connects a practical adult-learning objective to a language or rhetorical objective.

Examples include:

- comparing career sectors + comparison language;
- researching careers + direct speech, paraphrasing, and research organizers;
- career mapping + past simple/present perfect;
- résumé development + action verbs;
- informational interviewing + question formation;
- goal planning + conditionals;
- job-offer evaluation + hedging and peer review;
- workplace expectations + modals of obligation;
- workers' rights + transitions/connectors;
- debate + concession and connection language;
- further-education research + comparison, visual organizers, and textual evidence.

This architecture makes language practice consequential: learners use English while making decisions about work, education, family, rights, and future planning.

## Instructor enablement

The learning system depended on a large, rotating teaching workforce. Surviving orientation and teacher-development materials emphasize:

- Communicative Language Teaching;
- authentic production;
- scaffolding;
- intentional error correction;
- student interaction;
- Presentation–Practice–Production;
- task/project-based learning;
- differentiated instruction;
- translating real-world language use into classroom activities.

The goal was not only to give instructors content. It was to give them a usable model for adapting content without losing the learning objective.

## Iterative handoff and feedback

Archived Level 6 class-summary workbooks function as implementation records rather than static lesson plans. Their fields include:

- date;
- teacher(s);
- theme;
- topic;
- grammar/vocabulary/resources;
- alternatives;
- class reaction/mastery;
- additional exercises;
- comments for the next teacher.

This creates a lightweight curriculum feedback loop: instructors record what happened, what learners mastered, what needs repetition, and what the next instructor should know.

## Assessment and program learning

The role also included:

- literacy assessment administration;
- learner progress and retention analysis;
- program documentation;
- grant and compliance support;
- teacher observation and feedback.

The important design principle is that assessment data was connected to instructional and operational decisions rather than treated solely as a reporting requirement.

## What this case demonstrates

- curriculum architecture across a long multi-lesson sequence;
- contextualized adult learning;
- practical integration of language, career, education, and family goals;
- instructor onboarding and facilitator enablement;
- multi-site program operations;
- mixed-level and differentiated instruction;
- assessment and curriculum feedback;
- iterative handoff across instructors;
- program adaptation under disruption.

## Publication boundary

The original compiled curriculum incorporates external readings, transcripts, institutional resources, and other material whose authorship/rights vary. Raw teacher-observation records also contain identifiable information.

Accordingly, the public portfolio:

- does not republish the full original textbook;
- does not publish identifiable observation records;
- distinguishes curriculum architecture from ownership of third-party readings;
- uses newly authored summaries, maps, and derivative tools to demonstrate the design method.
