# Making and Knowing Project — Digital Humanities & Epistemic Design Case Study

## Scope

This case study documents a broader period of Making and Knowing Project participation, not only the final Epistemic Design group project.

Laura Elizabeth Hand worked regularly with the Making and Knowing Project for more than four months in Spring 2019 through Columbia University's interdisciplinary *Transforming Texts: Textual Analysis, Literary Modeling, and Visualization* course and associated lab, manuscript, and working-group activity. She remained an occasional contributor and participant after that intensive period.

The collaborative project with Sandra Goldstein Lehnert and Gregory Houser—*Material Qualities of the Page: A New Interface for the Digital Critical Edition*—was one capstone within that larger body of work.

## Project context

The Making and Knowing Project used the sixteenth-century French manuscript BnF Ms. Fr. 640 as a shared object for interdisciplinary research spanning textual scholarship, translation, material history, laboratory reconstruction, digital humanities, and digital-edition development.

The Project's Digital Critical Edition, *Secrets of Craft and Nature in Renaissance France*, later launched publicly in February 2020. This case study distinguishes Laura's documented student/collaborator work from the work of the core Making and Knowing team and the professional developers responsible for the published Edition.

## The work

### Sustained manuscript and digital-edition analysis

Across the Spring 2019 term, Laura repeatedly worked with Ms. Fr. 640 and its developing digital representations. Surviving coursework and correspondence document work involving:

- close reading and textual analysis;
- grammar, syntax, translation, and historical-language problems;
- computational and structured approaches to textual analysis;
- page structure and relationships among textual/material elements;
- the affordances and limitations of digital representation;
- questions about how interface choices shape interpretation.

This sustained work matters because the final interface proposal emerged from an extended period of working with the manuscript and Project methods rather than from a standalone design exercise.

### Lab and working-group participation

The record also documents recurring lab and Working Group activity connected to the Making and Knowing Project, including direct communication with Project staff, access to editorial markup and research resources, engagement with conservation questions, and participation in the Project's collaborative research environment.

### AR measurement concept: historically meaningful units

A separate, individually proposed idea from April 2019 explored how augmented reality could connect physical lab space with historically meaningful measurement systems.

Laura proposed combining a phone-camera measurement tool with the Project's space-mapping work so that a user could view dimensions not only in modern units, but in comparative early modern terms—for example, how many cannonballs wide or pans long an object or space might be. She also suggested that such a tool could later connect with other AR lab tools, including annotations.

A contemporaneous reply from Tianna Uchacz explicitly recognized the idea as an AR measurement tool that converts to early modern units.

**Evidence boundary:** the surviving record verifies Laura's independent concept, its connection to the space-mapping work, and the proposed relationship to existing AR annotation ideas. It does **not** currently verify that Laura built, implemented, or tested the annotation system itself. Those stronger claims should not be made unless separate evidence surfaces.

### Capstone: Material Qualities of the Page

The Epistemic Design group—Gregory Houser, Laura Elizabeth Hand, and Sandra Lehnert—proposed a new way to foreground the material qualities of a manuscript page within a digital critical edition.

The central design question was not simply how to digitize a page, but how an interface might help a reader treat the page itself as evidence.

The team explored:

- wireframed page regions;
- text blocks, headings, margins, images, markings, stains, and other material features;
- contextual information revealed through interaction;
- material and structural reading modes;
- improved searchability across page features;
- possible genetic readings showing the page through time;
- a time-lapse or related visual representation of page development.

## Laura's documented capstone role

The surviving project proposal describes Laura's role as including:

- technical knowledge related to graphic design and time-lapse imagery;
- epistemic design and wireframing;
- co-program management;
- textual insight.

The group correspondence also preserves work around the genetic/time-lapse component and confirms that the final deliverable was collaboratively developed and presented.

## Design hypothesis

The project treated the interface as an interpretive layer rather than a neutral container.

A conventional transcription can privilege linguistic content. A facsimile can preserve visual information while leaving structural and material features difficult to inspect. The proposed interface attempted to make those layers separately legible while keeping them related to the source page.

That design hypothesis can be expressed as:

> A digital edition should help readers inspect not only what a manuscript says, but how the material and structural organization of the page participates in meaning.

## Proposed interaction model

1. A reader selects a material/structural view.
2. Regions of the page become interactively legible.
3. A selected region foregrounds relevant information or commentary.
4. Users can move among textual, material, structural, and potentially chronological views.
5. A genetic view may expose possible stages in the history of a page.

The historical project used hover-oriented interaction language typical of the period. The 2026 derivative resource updates this approach by requiring keyboard, touch, non-hover, motion-reduced, and low-bandwidth alternatives.

## What was proposed vs. what was implemented

This distinction is essential.

The surviving materials support claims about research, analysis, wireframing, interface proposals, presentation, and prototype concepts. They do **not** support representing the student group's proposed feature as software Laura deployed into the published Digital Critical Edition.

The final public Edition was developed through the Making and Knowing Project's larger editorial and digital-development process. The Project's published documentation credits the core team, Columbia University Libraries, and Performant Software Solutions for the Edition's digital development.
