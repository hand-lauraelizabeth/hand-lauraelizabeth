# Modern Interview Preparation Checklist

A practical preparation system adapted and modernized from Laura Hand’s archived career-development materials. It retains useful preparation principles while removing rigid, dated, gendered, or one-size-fits-all interview rules.

## 1. Understand the role
- [ ] Re-read the posting and identify the 5–8 responsibilities or qualifications that appear most important.
- [ ] Mark the language the employer uses for those priorities.
- [ ] For each major requirement, identify at least one example from your experience that demonstrates related evidence.
- [ ] Identify any apparent gap or transition you may need to explain clearly.
- [ ] Prepare a one- or two-sentence answer to: Why this role, at this organization, now?

## 2. Research the organization
- [ ] Know what the organization does, whom it serves, and how this role contributes.
- [ ] Review current priorities, programs, products, or recent developments relevant to the role.
- [ ] Review the interviewer’s professional background when their identity has been provided.
- [ ] Note 2–3 specific things you genuinely want to understand better.
- [ ] Separate useful research from trivia: prioritize information that will improve your answers or questions.

## 3. Build an evidence bank
Prepare several examples rather than memorizing scripts. Collect stories that cover:
- [ ] A measurable accomplishment
- [ ] A difficult problem you solved
- [ ] A time you improved a process
- [ ] Collaboration or conflict
- [ ] Leadership or initiative
- [ ] Prioritization under competing demands
- [ ] Feedback you received and used
- [ ] A mistake, setback, or unexpected result and what changed afterward
- [ ] Learning something unfamiliar quickly
- [ ] Work relevant to the role’s most important requirement

For each example, know:
- **Situation / context:** What does the listener need to understand?
- **Task / problem:** What were you responsible for?
- **Action:** What did you specifically do?
- **Result:** What changed? Quantify where meaningful.
- **Reflection:** What did you learn or do differently afterward?

## 4. Prepare for common question types
Do not memorize word-for-word answers. Know the evidence you want available.

- [ ] **Tell me about yourself.** Focus on the professional through-line that makes the current opportunity logical.
- [ ] **Why this role / organization?** Connect your interests and evidence to something specific about the work.
- [ ] **What strength would you bring?** Choose a relevant strength and support it with evidence.
- [ ] **Tell me about a challenge / conflict / failure.** Spend less time describing the problem than explaining your actions, judgment, result, and learning.
- [ ] **Why are you leaving or considering leaving?** Answer accurately and professionally.
- [ ] **What is an area you are developing?** Use a genuine development area and explain what you are doing about it.
- [ ] **How do you prioritize?** Describe how you distinguish urgency, importance, dependencies, risk, and stakeholder needs.

## 5. Plan your questions
Prepare more questions than you expect to ask. Choose based on what the conversation has already answered.

Possible questions:
- [ ] What would success look like in the first 3–6 months?
- [ ] What problems or opportunities would this person inherit?
- [ ] How does the team make decisions and collaborate?
- [ ] What distinguishes someone who does especially well in this role?
- [ ] What priorities are most likely to change over the next year?
- [ ] What resources, systems, or stakeholders are central to the work?
- [ ] What does the remaining hiring process look like?

Compensation, working conditions, schedule, advancement, accessibility, and benefits are legitimate employment considerations. Timing and phrasing can vary by interview stage.

## 6. Check format and logistics
### In person
- [ ] Confirm address, building/access instructions, travel time, and contact information.
- [ ] Aim to arrive in the immediate area early without presenting yourself excessively early.
- [ ] Bring only materials that will actually be useful.

### Virtual
- [ ] Confirm platform/link and time zone.
- [ ] Test camera, microphone, headphones, connection, display name, and screen-sharing if relevant.
- [ ] Check lighting and what appears in frame.
- [ ] Close unnecessary applications and silence avoidable notifications.
- [ ] Keep the posting, résumé, and a short question list accessible without reading from a script.

### Phone
- [ ] Confirm who is calling whom.
- [ ] Choose a quiet location with reliable reception.
- [ ] Keep essential documents accessible.

## 7. Present yourself professionally—without performing a stereotype
- [ ] Choose clothing appropriate to the role, organization, interview format, and your own professional presentation.
- [ ] Prioritize neatness, comfort, accessibility, and the ability to focus on the conversation.
- [ ] Use attentive body language that is natural and workable for you.
- [ ] Do not force eye contact, handshakes, stillness, vocal patterns, clothing, grooming, or other behaviors merely to imitate a narrow model of professionalism.
- [ ] If you need an accommodation for the interview process, request it through the appropriate channel.

## 8. During the interview
- [ ] Listen to the entire question before answering.
- [ ] Ask for clarification when a question is ambiguous.
- [ ] Answer the question asked before adding context.
- [ ] Use specific examples rather than unsupported claims.
- [ ] Distinguish your individual contribution from the team’s work.
- [ ] Be concise, but give enough context for the evidence to make sense.
- [ ] It is acceptable to pause briefly to think.
- [ ] Take brief notes if useful and compatible with the format.
- [ ] If you realize you answered the wrong question, correct course directly.

## 9. Close the conversation
- [ ] Ask your remaining high-value questions.
- [ ] If appropriate, ask whether there is anything in your background you can clarify or expand upon.
- [ ] Confirm next steps and expected timing.
- [ ] If you remain interested, say so plainly.
- [ ] Thank the interviewer for their time.

## 10. After the interview
Record:
- [ ] Interviewers and roles
- [ ] Questions asked
- [ ] Examples you used
- [ ] Questions you wish you had answered differently
- [ ] New information about the role/team
- [ ] Concerns or uncertainties
- [ ] Commitments or materials you promised to send
- [ ] Follow-up date / next-step timing

Send a concise thank-you or follow-up when appropriate. Personalize it to the conversation rather than sending a generic template.

## 11. Evaluate the opportunity, too
Afterward ask:
- [ ] Did the actual responsibilities match the posting?
- [ ] Were expectations and decision rights reasonably clear?
- [ ] How did interviewers describe workload, management, collaboration, and priorities?
- [ ] Did different interviewers give contradictory answers?
- [ ] What information do I still need before making a decision?
- [ ] Did I observe anything that changes my interest in the role?

## Quick 15-minute version
1. Read the posting again and identify the top three needs.
2. Prepare three evidence-rich examples that map to those needs.
3. Research the organization’s current work and the interviewer(s).
4. Prepare three thoughtful questions.
5. Verify logistics/technology and have your résumé and posting accessible.

## Development note
This 2026 resource is a modernized derivative developed from archived interview-preparation and career-development materials in Laura Hand’s files. The archive contains useful preparation principles alongside advice that is dated, overly rigid, gendered, or context-dependent. This version intentionally preserves the useful system—research, evidence, practice, questions, logistics, and follow-up—without reproducing those limitations.
