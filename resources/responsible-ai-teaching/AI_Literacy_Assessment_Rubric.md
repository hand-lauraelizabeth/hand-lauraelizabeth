# AI Literacy & Responsible Use Assessment Rubric

This rubric evaluates whether a learner can make sound choices around AI-assisted work. It does **not** reward prompt length, jargon, or the appearance of technical sophistication.

Score each dimension from 0–3.

| Dimension | 3 — Strong | 2 — Developing | 1 — Limited | 0 — Missing / unsafe to assess |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Task choice** | Explains why AI is or is not appropriate; considers a non-AI alternative and consequence level. | Identifies a reasonable AI role but gives limited consideration to alternatives or stakes. | Uses AI by default without explaining why. | Delegates a consequential task without examining whether AI belongs in it. |
| **Prompt specification** | Task, audience/context, constraints, boundaries, and output contract are explicit and proportionate. | Most requirements are present but one or more important assumptions remain implicit. | Prompt communicates a general goal but leaves multiple material assumptions unstated. | Request is too vague or contradictory to evaluate reliably. |
| **Evidence use** | Factual claims are tied to an explicit evidence boundary; inference and unsupported claims are distinguished. | Uses relevant evidence but occasionally blurs source fact and inference. | Evidence is present but treated loosely or selectively. | Relies on unsupported model output as evidence. |
| **Verification** | Identifies what must be checked, how it will be checked, and what happens if verification fails. | Includes some checking but not for all material claims or calculations. | Says to “fact check” without an executable method. | No verification plan for material output. |
| **Privacy / data minimization** | Uses only necessary information; addresses authorization, sensitivity, retention, and exposure where relevant. | Recognizes sensitivity but minimization or authorization is incomplete. | Notices privacy only after information has been included. | Shares unnecessary sensitive/confidential data or ignores obvious restrictions. |
| **Risk and differential impact** | Identifies scenario-specific harms, affected groups, and plausible failure paths; does not rely on a generic ethics list. | Identifies relevant risks but analysis remains broad. | Names abstract principles without connecting them to the workflow. | Assumes absence of obvious harm means the system is safe. |
| **Human responsibility** | Names the accountable human role, review point, override/appeal path, and what the AI may not decide. | Human review is present but authority or timing is unclear. | “Human in the loop” is stated without meaningful control. | AI output effectively becomes the decision with no accountable review. |
| **Provenance / documentation** | Material inputs, sources, prompts/instructions, tool/version context, and decisions can be reconstructed where appropriate. | Most material information is documented but gaps remain. | Documentation is informal or incomplete. | Important outputs cannot be traced to their inputs or process. |
| **Reflection and redesign** | Uses observed limitations or failures to change the prompt, workflow, or decision rule. | Identifies limitations and proposes partial improvements. | Reflects on the output but not the surrounding system. | Treats first-pass output as final or equates fluency with quality. |

## Suggested interpretation

Do not collapse the rubric into a single “safe / unsafe” score.

A learner may be strong in prompt design and weak in evidence use, or strong in ethics language and weak in operational controls. Those differences are instructionally important.

If a total is useful for grading, use it only as a summary:

- **22–27:** consistently strong evidence of AI literacy and responsible-use reasoning;
- **16–21:** functional but uneven; identify the lowest dimensions for revision;
- **9–15:** substantial gaps; require redesign before relying on the workflow;
- **0–8:** insufficient evidence of responsible use.

For consequential scenarios, a high total should **not** compensate for a zero in privacy/data minimization, verification, or human responsibility.

## Short-form learner reflection

After completing an AI-assisted task, ask:

1. What did I delegate to AI?
2. What evidence did the output rely on?
3. What did I verify independently?
4. What did I choose not to share?
5. What remained my decision?
6. What changed after I reviewed the first output?
7. What would make me use a non-AI process next time?

## Instructor use

Use the rubric with:
- prompt-repair exercises;
- research synthesis;
- data-analysis narratives;
- AI-assisted feedback;
- workplace workflow redesign;
- the applied capstone in the [Prompt Literacy & Responsible AI Teaching Kit](README.md).

The rubric is an educational assessment tool, not a legal, compliance, or safety certification.
