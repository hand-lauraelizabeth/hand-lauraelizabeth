# Instructor Leadership as Curriculum Infrastructure
## Columbia Community Impact — Teacher Coordination
**Author-review case study · unpublished · 9 November 2026**

### The decision
A teaching program is not sustained by a curriculum packet alone. It depends on who teaches, how instructors are prepared, how expectations are communicated, and what happens when the classroom reveals a gap between a plan and its implementation. At Columbia Community Impact, I was responsible for the teacher-facing work that connected those decisions. I served as Teacher Coordinator, reporting to the Director, and supervised on-site coordinators while overseeing instructor selection, training, documentation, and regulatory requirements.

### My responsibility
The position required more than delivering orientation. I handled the teacher-related functions of the program: selecting and preparing instructors, writing up relevant information, maintaining the required processes, and providing a point of accountability when classroom practice needed attention. The Director was the only role above mine in this reporting structure. That distinction matters because instructor support was not an isolated contribution to a larger process; the process itself was part of my responsibility.

### Method: a continuous instructional system
I approached teacher coordination as a connected sequence rather than a series of administrative transactions. Selection established the capabilities and expectations instructors would bring into classrooms. Training translated those expectations into teachable practice. Written guidance and records helped keep the program consistent across instructors and sites. Oversight of on-site coordinators created a route for information from implementation to reach the person responsible for the teaching system.

The sequence also worked in reverse. What instructors and coordinators encountered in practice could identify where guidance needed clarification or where preparation needed to be more explicit. That feedback relationship is central to my later curriculum-development work: instructional quality depends on both the design of learning and the operational conditions under which teaching takes place.

### What the work produced
My contribution was an instructor-management and support function spanning preparation, documentation, compliance, and on-site coordination. The record supports the scope of responsibility described here; it does not yet establish a defensible numerical claim about teacher counts, learner gains, retention, or changes in instructional quality. I would not substitute a program-wide outcome for a result attributable to my decisions.

### What this case demonstrates
The through-line is operational as well as pedagogical. I treated instructor readiness, implementation, and accountability as parts of curriculum quality, rather than as work that begins after curriculum design ends. That approach continues to inform how I structure facilitator guidance, course review, and learning-program handoffs.
