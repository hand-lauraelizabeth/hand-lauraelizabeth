Learning context

Guidance & worked example: Title and scope

Name the actual learning experience, not a broad subject. Example: “90-minute workshop: Making defensible project recommendations,” rather than “Project Management.”

Guidance & worked example: Learner analysis

Example: “12 new program coordinators; mixed Excel experience; comfortable discussing risk, less practiced in turning constraints into decision criteria.” Separate observed needs from assumptions.

Guidance & worked example: Real delivery constraints

Example: “90-minute virtual workshop, one facilitator, captioned slides, accessible worksheet, chat-based pairs, and a 10-minute independent task.” Be realistic about time.

Guidance & worked example: Accessibility and options

Example: “Provide a tagged-text project brief, an accessible criteria table, high-contrast visuals, transcripts, and written participation options. Never rely on color alone.”

Design around the real task

Guidance & worked example: Authentic performance

Strong: “Select one of two program designs and defend the choice using budget, risk, and beneficiary needs.” Weak: “Understand program strategy.”

Guidance & worked example: Purpose outside the classroom

Example: “The recommendation goes to a director who must allocate staff capacity. Unexamined assumptions could create cost overruns or exclude participants.”

Guidance & worked example: Observable evidence

Example: “A one-page decision memo with a comparison table, a recommendation, two acknowledged uncertainties, and an explanation of tradeoffs.”

Guidance & worked example: Quality standards

Example: “Uses at least three explicit criteria; cites the provided evidence correctly; addresses two risks; identifies the condition that would change the recommendation.”

Backward-design trace

Authentic task → evidence of success → necessary knowledge and skills → supported practice → independent performance → feedback and revision → transfer.

Write a learning objective

Use the context, an observable cognitive action, the knowledge or skill to be demonstrated, and the evidence that will show success.

Guidance & worked example: Choose a cognitive process

Choose the level of performance the learner must demonstrate, not the sophistication of the topic. “Evaluate” fits defending a decision using criteria; “Remember” fits identifying terminology.

Guidance & worked example: Use an observable action

Strong verbs include identify, compare, calculate, diagnose, justify, design. Avoid unobservable outcomes such as know, appreciate, become familiar with, or understand.

Guidance & worked example: Specify the content or skill

Strong: “two program-delivery options using cost, access, and risk criteria.” Too broad: “the topic” or “leadership skills.”

Guidance & worked example: Set a meaningful condition

Example: “given two fictional project briefs, a budget ceiling, and an accessibility checklist.” Include resources and constraints that matter to authentic performance.

Guidance & worked example: Define the assessment artifact

Example: “A recommendation memo scored against a four-part rubric: evidence, criteria, risks, and decision rationale.” An attendance poll alone is not adequate evidence of this outcome.

Guidance & worked example: Make practice match the assessment

Example: “First annotate a model comparison, then critique a flawed one in pairs, then independently decide a new case.” Give corrective feedback before the final attempt.

Practice and assessment sequence

Move from noticing and interpretation toward supported practice, independent performance, and transfer. Each step should contribute to at least one objective.

Example three-session sequence and alignment check

20 minutes: interpret a fictional project brief and identify decision criteria; evidence: annotated criteria table. 30 minutes: compare two options in pairs and receive targeted feedback; evidence: tradeoff matrix. 40 minutes: independently recommend an option on a new scenario; evidence: decision memo. Link each session to the outcome it supports and include a genuine assessment, not just attendance.

Feedback and transfer

Guidance & worked example: Make feedback actionable

Example: “After a peer-review round, learners revise one unsupported assertion and add one missing risk to their memo.” Specify when feedback occurs and what it can change.

Guidance & worked example: Plan an independent transfer task

Example: “Apply the same comparison criteria to a new case with different budget and staffing constraints, without a worked example.”

Guidance & worked example: Check prerequisites without excluding learners

Example: “Ask learners to interpret a short evidence table before the workshop. Provide a one-page primer for those unfamiliar with cost and risk language.”

Instructor handoff and revision

Guidance & worked example: Write usable facilitator instructions

Example: “Distribute accessible briefs before the workshop; reserve 15 minutes for independent justification; do not replace the final task with lecture.”

Guidance & worked example: Name accountability

Example: “Program lead; review each term and when source requirements change.” A person's role is more durable than their name alone.

Guidance & worked example: Define a revision trigger

Example: “If learners consistently omit limitations, revise the model and add a targeted comparison exercise; document the change after two deliveries.”

Alignment review

These are completeness and wording checks, not a substitute for discipline-specific or accessibility review.

What a strong alignment review looks like

Trace every objective to an activity, an observable assessment, and explicit success criteria. For the project-decision example, “justify a recommendation” is assessed by a decision memo—not attendance or recall alone. Identify objectives with no assessment, unsupported final tasks, missing transfer opportunities, and access barriers. Review the output as a professional, not an automated accreditation or quality score.
